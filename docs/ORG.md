# Northern Motors: the company, the people, the vehicles

Written 2026-11-03 from the author's decisions: "Make a complete org chart,
with a CEO, a COO, a product manager (on the engineer side), also include
service, engineer (design, manufacturing, software), and sales departments.
The company makes 6 different vehicles, 3 are specific vehicles, each with a
variation model."

Everything here is fiction. The author named the company ("The name is
Northern Motors") and accepted the cast ("The names look good"). Every person named is invented for this tool; none is a real
person, and the names deliberately avoid anyone in the author's working life.

## The company in one paragraph

Northern Motors builds complete vehicles in one plant: three vehicle
lines, each with a variant, about six vehicles a week, sold to municipalities,
utilities, contractors, rural fleets and fire departments across the prairies.
Everything but the tires and the glass is made in the building, from cut steel
to a vehicle that drives out the door. Ninety-two people. It grew from a
twelve-person fabrication shop, and most of the people who ran it at twelve
still run it at ninety-two, which is both why it works and why it strains.
It is a Stage Two company in Northbound's terms: too big for everyone to know
everything, not yet built so that they do not have to.

### What each part of the chart does, and where it strains

- **The CEO** founded the company as a fabrication shop and still walks the
  floor most mornings. He knows every long-tenure employee by name and still
  gives direct instructions to supervisors when he sees something, which is
  the single biggest source of confused authority in the building.
- **The COO** was hired three years ago to put structure in. She owns the plant
  and service. Her standing problem is that half the plant still routes around
  her to the CEO.
- **The Plant Manager** came up through assembly. He is good on the floor and
  spends too much time there; his two operations managers get less of him
  than they need.
- **The Operations Managers** each cover three or four departments. Metal is
  the upstream half of the plant, Finishing the downstream half, and the
  handoff between them (paint to electrical) is where the two managers'
  priorities collide.
- **The Scheduler** is the one person who knows how the schedule actually gets
  built. When she is away, the plant runs on last week's plan.
- **Quality** reports to the plant manager, not to engineering, which means a
  recurring defect is a production problem first and a design problem only
  when someone insists.
- **Maintenance** is two people for a plant that has a press, a CNC, a paint
  booth, a crane and forty years of mixed equipment.
- **Materials & Logistics** buys steel, tires, glass and electrical components,
  receives, stores and ships. A late tire delivery stops assembly; nobody
  upstream notices until the yard fills with vehicles on stands.
- **Service** fixes vehicles in the field and in the shop, handles warranty
  and parts. Service sees every design and build defect six months after the
  plant does, and the plant rarely hears about it.
- **The Director of Engineering** runs three small teams and the product
  manager. Engineering is where change comes from, and the plant experiences
  change as disruption.
- **The Product Manager** sits on the engineering side and owns the six
  vehicles: what each one is, what the variants include, what sales may
  promise, what engineering changes and when. She is the interface between
  sales promises and plant capacity, and she is outnumbered.
- **Design Engineering** designs the vehicles and the running changes. **
  Manufacturing Engineering** owns process, tooling, fixtures and standard
  work; the CI/Lean coordinator sits here with a dotted line to the plant
  manager. **Software Engineering** writes the vehicle firmware, the
  diagnostics and the telematics; its work lands in the electrical department
  as harness and controller changes.
- **Sales** sells to fleets and municipalities on relationships and lead time.
  A rep's promise of a delivery date is the most common way a rush order
  enters the plant.
- **The Controller** runs finance, HR and administration with two people. HR
  is one coordinator; discipline documentation goes through her, and she is
  careful about it.

### The pipeline gaps the scenarios draw on

- The Operations Manager, Metal, on leave for a month; the plant manager
  covers and is on the floor even more.
- The paint supervisor quits with two weeks' notice; nobody was being
  developed.
- The scheduler away; the plant runs on an old plan and nobody owns the new
  one.
- Machining's one operator who knows the CNC program.
- A lead who is the de facto supervisor and has been for a year.
- The CEO giving a supervisor an instruction the operations manager does not
  know about.

## The vehicles

Three lines, each with a variant: six vehicles. The mix is the plant's real
scheduling problem, because the variants load the departments differently.

| Line | Base model | Variant | Who buys it | What the variant changes in the plant |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Work truck | **Drayman**: regular cab, long flat deck | **Drayman Crew**: four-door crew cab, short deck | Rural contractors, municipal works, farms | More welding (the cab), more interior; the deck is simpler |
| Van | **Hauler**: cargo van | **Hauler 9**: nine-seat passenger | Trades, delivery, community transport, care homes | Interior-heavy (seating, trim, rear HVAC), more electrical |
| Field vehicle | **Surveyor**: 4x4 field vehicle | **Surveyor Rescue**: fire and EMS fit-out | Utilities, oil and gas, rural fire departments | Electrical and software heavy (lighting, radios, controllers), custom interior, the longest build in the plant |

A typical week: two Drayman, one Drayman Crew, one Hauler, one Hauler 9, one
Surveyor. A Surveyor Rescue ships two or three times a month and is the
vehicle the plant manager asks about every morning. **The mix moves the
bottleneck:** a Drayman-heavy week makes welding the constraint, a Hauler 9
and Rescue week makes electrical and interior the constraint, and a plan that
balances the departments is a plan sales did not promise. This is the
systems-thinking mechanic for the CI/Lean track and the group mode, and it is
why the welding supervisor's week depends on decisions made in sales.
