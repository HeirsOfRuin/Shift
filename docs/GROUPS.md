# Group sessions: the facilitator's copy

Six sessions a coach runs with a room, each built on a module everyone plays
on their own phone with the same seed. The source of truth is
`src/data/groups.data.js`, shown on the coach's screen under *Group sessions*;
this page is the printable copy and is stale if they disagree. Where they
come from and why they are built this way: `FRAME-coach.md`, section 3.

Every session: a flip chart, four to ten people, the coach asks and does not
answer, and a commitment each person reads aloud. The reveal is for the
facilitator and stays folded until the debrief.

## The round board

The plane exercise without the pieces: every group runs the same eight rounds in the welding bay, and the flip chart shows who changed one rule and read the next round, and who changed three and found out why the exercise says not to.

- **Played:** Round two. **Size:** 4 to 10, in pairs or threes, one phone each group. **Time:** 90 minutes.
- **From:** The flow rounds (the plane exercise); Handbook 2, urgency and strategy

| Min | Block | The facilitator |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | Frame | One rule for the room: nobody opens the brief until everyone has it. Groups of two or three, one phone, the same seed (the coach reads it out; the brief shows it). Say the three rules out loud: rack or batch, push or pull, check at the end or at each station. Do not say which matters. |
| 35 | Rounds 1 to 8 | Each group plays a week, then calls out its board: bodies, work waiting, found late, what changed. Write every group's line on the flip chart before anyone plays the next week. Do not comment on the lines. Note which group changed what in week one. |
| 15 | Read the chart | Ask the room, not the groups: which line dipped in week two, and why? Which group changed the most and shipped the least? Which group read a round before changing anything? Where did a rule slide back by Friday, and what does that say about the crew? |
| 20 | Debrief | The learning dip: a change costs the first week, two changes cost the first two. Then the question that matters: what is the batch of six on your floor? Where do you push instead of pull? What is checked at the end that could be checked where it is made? |
| 10 | Commit | One rule each person will change on their own floor in the next two weeks, and what they will read first to know whether it worked. Read aloud. The coach names one pattern seen in the room and closes on time. |

**Debrief questions**

1. Which rule did you change first, and what told you to?
2. What did the dip in week two cost you, and was it worth it?
3. When a rule slid back by Friday, whose change was it?
4. On your floor, what is the rack of six?

**The reveal (facilitator only).** The room reaches for all three rules at once because each sounds right. One cause was drawn; only one rule fixed it; the other two cost a dip for nothing. The group that read the first round and changed one rule usually ships the most. If a group found the cause and still lost, the rule slid back: the crew had not taken it. That is a change the supervisor made for them, not with them.

**The commitment card.** One rule I will change on my floor, and what I will read first to know if it worked:

## Why it lands in paint

The systems-thinking scenario, played instead of read: each group runs paint for eight weeks on the receiving end of welding's work, then explains to the room why the pressure keeps landing there without blaming anyone.

- **Played:** The other side. **Size:** 4 to 10, groups of three or four, one phone each. **Time:** 120 minutes.
- **From:** The author's systems-thinking simulation; Handbook 7, 4 and 1

| Min | Block | The facilitator |
|---|---|---|
| 15 | Frame | Say it in the first minute: this is not a problem-solving session. You are not here to fix paint. You are here to understand why pressure keeps showing up where it does. Groups of three, one phone, the same seed. Hand out the five questions (below) on paper; they answer them after the run, not during. |
| 40 | Play | Each group plays The other side to the end. The coach circulates and does not help. Note what each group does with Theresa's handover note, and whether anyone goes back to welding before week four. |
| 25 | The five questions | Flip chart per group: Where does pressure pile up in this bay, and why does it show up in paint? What work is being done that nobody's job says they own? Who benefits from it working this way? If Theresa were away another month, what would fail first? What problem is management trying to solve but has not named right? |
| 10 | Slow it down | Questions only, never a correction: Who owns that decision on a bad day? What breaks if that person is not there? What behaviour does this bay quietly reward? Silence is fine. |
| 20 | Reveal | Each group presents five minutes. Then the layers, in order: who absorbs the load (the senior painter, the supervisor who reorders to protect assembly), authority against ownership (paint owns the outcome and not the cause), what information actually flows (verbal, late, emotional), the fragility test, the illusion that it is a capacity problem, and the lever: who owns "paint-ready" before paint. |
| 10 | Commit | One behaviour each person will stop tolerating under pressure, in their words, on the card. The coach keeps the cards and asks about them in thirty days. |

**Debrief questions**

1. Where does the pressure pile up, and why does it show up in paint?
2. What work is being done that nobody's job says they own?
3. Who benefits from the bay working this way today?
4. If the senior painter were away for two weeks, what would fail first?
5. What problem is management trying to solve but has not named right?

**The reveal (facilitator only).** Expect the room to diagnose paint's capacity, propose an inspection step, suggest pushing back harder, and recommend training welders. These are the signals, not mistakes; note which each group reaches for first. The hidden thing is that no role owns what paint-ready means, so paint discovers defects late, fixes them quietly, and absorbs the variation. Paint owns outcomes without owning causes. Until "paint-ready" is owned outside paint, paint stays a buffer.

**The commitment card.** One behaviour I will stop tolerating under pressure:

## Whose call is it

The leadership tier finds out it does not share a model of whose decision is whose: everyone plays Nobody upstairs alone, then the room votes on each call before the game says what it cost.

- **Played:** Nobody upstairs. **Size:** 4 to 8, each on their own phone. **Time:** 90 minutes.
- **From:** The author's exercise "Whose job is it?"; Handbook 4 and 8

| Min | Block | The facilitator |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | Frame | Everyone plays alone, same seed, phones face down between weeks. The rule: when a decision card comes up, stop. Do not pick. Wait for the room. |
| 45 | Play, one card at a time | At each decision card the coach reads it out and asks for hands: yours to make, or not yours? Count and write it on the chart. Then everyone picks what they want and plays the week. Friday is read in silence. Repeat. Expect the biggest split on the Saturday call and the scrap call. |
| 15 | Where we did not agree | Go back over the chart. Every card where the hands split is a finding: this tier does not agree on who owns that call. Do not settle it in the room. Write it on a sheet titled "Ownership we do not agree on"; it goes to the manager. |
| 10 | The cost | What did guessing cost, either way: the call made that was not yours, and the call not made that was? Who in the room went up the chain, who went around it, and what happened when Dana came back? |
| 10 | Commit | One decision each person has been making that belongs to someone else, or one they have been sending up that is theirs. Named, with a date. Read aloud. |

**Debrief questions**

1. Which call split the room, and why?
2. What did you do with a call that was not yours, and what did it cost?
3. Who did you tell, and when?
4. On your floor, what decision is understood but never written down?

**The reveal (facilitator only).** Role confusion is a design problem, not a personality one. The split hands are the result: a tier that disagrees on who owns the scrap call will have operators who have already found the gap. The sheet of disagreements is the deliverable; the manager decides them, not the room.

**The commitment card.** One decision I will stop making, or start making, because of whose it is:

## Same floor, different rules

Every supervisor plays the same rush week, then the room finds out at which shortcut each of them gave, and agrees one standard where there were six.

- **Played:** Rush week. **Size:** 4 to 8 supervisors and leads, each on their own phone. **Time:** 90 minutes.
- **From:** The author's peer-alignment check; Handbook 7, culture under pressure

| Min | Block | The facilitator |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | Frame | Alone, same seed, no talking. Before anyone plays, each writes on paper: the quality defect I send back versus let through with a note; the standard for an unexcused absence; when I escalate a performance issue. Fold it; it comes out later. |
| 35 | Play | Rush week to the end. The coach notes nothing; the game keeps the log. |
| 20 | The shortcut list | On the chart, one row per shortcut the module offers (the tack check, lunch, the sleeves, the spatter, the gauges, the Saturdays). Each person says what they did at each. Where the room splits, that is the conversation. Then unfold the papers and read the three answers around the room, one question at a time. |
| 15 | Agree one | For each split, ask the room to agree one standard now if it can, and record who takes the ones it cannot to the manager. The agreed standards go on a sheet the coach keeps. |
| 10 | Commit | One standard each person will hold through the next busy week, and how the crew will know. Read aloud. |

**Debrief questions**

1. Which shortcut did you take, and what were you telling yourself at the time?
2. Where did your answer differ from the person beside you?
3. What does the crew already know about the gaps between you?
4. What is the rule in June?

**The reveal (facilitator only).** Operators find the gaps between supervisors and use them, not out of malice: inconsistency makes room. The biggest spread is usually on the quality defect question. Whatever slides in the rush is the rule by June; the standard is what you hold on the worst week.

**The commitment card.** One standard I will hold through the next rush, and how my crew will know:

## The same fire

The handbook's first module as a run: each pair plays Firefighting, and the room compares the hours spent putting it out against the hours it would have taken to find out why it keeps lighting.

- **Played:** Firefighting. **Size:** 4 to 6, pairs. **Time:** 90 minutes.
- **From:** Handbook 1, firefighting is a leadership failure

| Min | Block | The facilitator |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | Open | Pre-work read out, one minute each: two problems on your floor that came back three or more times in six months. Write them on the chart. No discussion. |
| 30 | Play | Pairs, same seed. One holds the phone, the other argues. Swap at week four. |
| 20 | Count the hours | Each pair reads its log: hours spent on the fire each week, hours spent looking for why. Total both columns on the chart. Then: at which week did you stop and look, if you did, and what made you? |
| 15 | Back to the chart | Return to the morning's list of recurring problems. For each: what has actually been changed about the conditions that cause it? If the answer is nothing, it was postponed, not solved. |
| 15 | Commit | One recurring problem each person will solve at the root this month, and one they will stop solving themselves and hand to a named owner. Read aloud; the room asks how it will see it. |

**Debrief questions**

1. How many hours did the fire take across the run, and how many did finding the cause take?
2. What were you doing the week the fix did not hold?
3. If you were off for two weeks, which fires would start first?
4. Where are you the single point everything routes through?

**The reveal (facilitator only).** Firefighting rewards reaction. It feels important, gives visible wins, and lets a leader avoid the uncomfortable cause. The run makes the ledger visible: the fire costs two hours a week forever, the cause costs three hours once. If a problem keeps returning, it was not solved; it was postponed.

**The commitment card.** One recurring problem I will solve at the root this month, and one I will hand to a named owner:

## Where the week went

Everyone plays the first module, then lays their forty hours on the chart: what was planned, what the floor took, what was refused, and what was never going to fit.

- **Played:** The week. **Size:** 4 to 8, each on their own phone. **Time:** 90 minutes.
- **From:** Handbook 2, when everything is urgent nothing is strategic

| Min | Block | The facilitator |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | Open | Pre-work: last week, in two numbers, reactive hours against planned. Round the room, numbers only. Below twenty percent reactive from a supervisor is usually wrong; ask for the example. |
| 35 | Play | The week, alone, same seed. The coach asks for the hour strip at the end of each week: over forty or not, and what pushed it. |
| 20 | The chart | Four columns per person: planned, the floor, refused, never fit. Fill them from the run. Then the inbox: who said no to the wire rep, the town hall, the gas supplier, and who put off one of their own people instead. |
| 15 | Protect it | What would you stop doing this week to protect two hours? Who on your crew absorbs the urgency so you do not have to, and is that sustainable for them? |
| 10 | Commit | One improvement each person has been putting off until things calm down, scheduled for a day and time inside two weeks. Read aloud. |

**Debrief questions**

1. What pushed your week over, and was it a choice?
2. What did you refuse, and what did refusing cost?
3. What did you put off that belonged to one of your own people?
4. Which hour this week was the important thing that was not urgent?

**The reveal (facilitator only).** Urgency creates pressure to act; strategy needs permission to pause. The run shows refusal as a decision: the person who said no to the wire rep had an hour the person who said yes did not. Today's urgency is usually last month's neglected system.

**The commitment card.** The improvement I will schedule this week, with its day and time:
